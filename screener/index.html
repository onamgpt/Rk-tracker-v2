What was filtered out, and why

The rejects matter as much as the picks. Every company the screen touched and excluded is listed here under the reason it failed. Tap a reason to see the names. A company can fail several tests; it appears under each.

The two layers

Layer 1 — Selection (this page). Which companies belong in an AIM programme at all: solvency, accounting quality, liquidity, governance, and whether the price series oscillates enough to give AIM anything to work on.

Layer 2 — AIM (the trading app). Mechanical position sizing on whatever survives Layer 1. AIM decides how much to buy or sell as price moves. It does not pick stocks and never has.

Keeping these apart is the whole point. A screen result is a candidate for consideration, not an instruction to trade.

Two scores, deliberately not merged

Company quality (0–100) is built only from measures with published out-of-sample support: Altman Z for distress, Piotroski F for fundamental strength, leverage, cash-flow quality and accruals. It looks at the business and ignores the share price entirely.

AIM suitability (0–100) asks a different question: would AIM have had work to do here? It scores completed drawdown-and-recovery round trips, realised volatility, how deep the typical recovered fall went, and how long recovery took — and penalises sustained one-directional decline.

A superb company that grinds steadily upward scores badly on the second axis, and it should: AIM earns from round trips, not from being right about the business. Both numbers are always shown separately.

The combined figure used for ordering is the geometric mean of the two. That is deliberate: an average would let a brilliant score on one axis paper over a poor score on the other, which is exactly the trade this screen refuses. A zero on either axis cannot be rescued.

No score can overturn a gate. Gates run first and are absolute. An insolvent company with perfect oscillation is rejected, not ranked.

Where the screen performs badly

Written down in advance rather than discovered later.

  • Regime change. Drawdown-and-recovery history is measured over three years. A company whose past recoveries came from conditions that no longer hold will still score well. History of bouncing is not a promise of bouncing.
  • Everything correlating at once. The rebalancing premium AIM harvests depends on positions moving independently. In a broad market panic they do not, and the screen cannot see that coming.
  • Annual fundamentals. Solvency data is yearly. A company can deteriorate for three quarters before this screen notices.
  • Value traps. The screen rewards a strong company trading low in its range. It cannot always distinguish that from a company that is cheap because it deserves to be.
  • Thin evidence on some inputs. Promoter holding is a judgement call rather than a validated factor. It is weighted lightly and is not a gate.
  • A measure that was tried and dropped. A variance ratio was built in as a mean-reversion statistic and removed after testing: on a perfectly oscillating series it reported "strongly trending", and the reading flipped depending on an arbitrary horizon setting. Round-trip counts are used instead because they measure the same thing in units that mean something.

Where AIM itself performs badly

AIM's documented weakness is a sustained one-directional decline. It keeps buying as price falls, and the cash pool drains.

Your own rules sharpen this. With a single shared cash pool, one falling holding can consume cash that other positions need. With sells blocked below average cost, a position under water cannot be trimmed at all — so the usual release valve is shut. The combination has a specific failure state: fully invested, cash pool empty, nothing sellable, and no capacity to act on the next fall.

The screen mitigates this by rejecting falling knives and structural decliners, and by penalising negative drift on the suitability axis. It does not eliminate it. Nothing at selection time can.

Survivorship bias — read before trusting any backtest

Both universes were built from companies that exist today: 699 Indian names from July 2026, 434 US names from August 2026. Every company that failed, was delisted or was acquired at a discount in the years before is simply absent.

Any backtest run over these lists is therefore measuring a world in which the losers were removed in advance. Published estimates put the inflation at roughly 1–4% a year for large-cap screens and higher for beaten-down mid-caps — which is precisely the kind of company this screen looks for, so the effect here sits at the worse end.

This is not fixable with the data available. It is a ceiling on what any backtest here can tell you, not a floor. Treat a backtested return as an optimistic bound and nothing more.

No parameters in this screen were tuned to make a backtest look good. The thresholds are stated with reasons in the code and were fixed before any results were produced.

Judgement calls, labelled as such

  • 20% as the fall size that counts as an AIM round trip, and 60% of the way back as recovery.
  • 30–70% annualised volatility as the working band.
  • Promoter holding as a governance proxy for India.
  • The weights on both axes. They are reasoned, not fitted.

These are defensible, not proven. They were chosen for stated reasons and left alone.

What this screen does not do

  • It does not read the live apps' data or write to them. It keeps its own results under its own storage key.
  • It does not rebuild the shared fundamentals tables. It reads them if the trading app has populated them and says so plainly if they are missing or stale.
  • It does not value companies. Nothing here says a price is cheap.
  • It is not advice. It is a filter over public data, built for one person's own money.
